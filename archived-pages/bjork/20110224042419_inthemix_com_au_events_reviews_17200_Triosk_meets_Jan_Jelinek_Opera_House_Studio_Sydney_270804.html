Triosk meets Jan Jelinek @ Opera House Studio, Sydney (27/08/04)

Poor old jazz music. It used to be the sound of the night – go back 80 years and people like us were disregarding prohibition, drinking whiskey and smoking pot and grooving away to live jams in surreptitious basement dives, hiding from the cops and the onset of dawn. Plenty of parallels with our culture. Nowadays, however, jazz seems to be the province of either the elderly or those classical music fans who consider themselves a bit avant-garde and with-it. Which is quite a shame.


Sydney’s decided it’s time it had a jazz festival, and so the inaugaural Jazz:Now fiesta was held at the Opera House Studio during last week. In truth there’s not too much about contemporary jazz that excites me – I find it hard to reconcile the chin-stroking attitude that pervades the genre nowadays with the marijuana-induced frenzies that accompanied its inception, but my waning interest was once more aroused upon hearing that Jan Jelinek was coming to town. Not widely known in jazz circles, Jelinek is an electronic producer of note, producing sublimely textured micro-house that sounds like a hybrid of Akufen, Daniel Bell and Moodymann.


For this gig, he was teamed up with Sydney three-piece Triosk, with whom he’s recently released an album (“1+3+1” on ~scape), for what would be their first live performance together – indeed, their album was the result of a distant collaboration, with Jelinek in Europe and the instrumentalists in Sydney, their interaction being fractured by the snail mail system. Nonetheless, as an avowed Jelinek fan, I was keen to see what would ensue.


But first I had to pay a price.


Supporting Triosk & Jelinek were a couple of local acts – the Freedivers and the Gang of Five. Exponents of abstract, freeform jazz, which relies as more on soundscapes than it does on structure, both acts were tedious to say the least. I’ve heard (and enjoyed) my fair share of abstract, atonal, downright bizarre jazz music but these folk offered nothing new or even exciting. It was mediocre, self-indulgent, boring and at times risible. There was no denying the technical proficiency of any of the musicians on stage but I found it virtually impossible to connect with either of the acts and their music on any level beyond the super-critical. Like badly-made soft porn, it was one giant, protracted, seemingly endless and ultimately unfulfilling masturbation.


It was with a sense of anxiety that I witnessed Triosk and Jelinek take to the stage. To the accompaniment of grainy black and white footage looking out over an aeroplane’s wing high above the cloud (perhaps evoking the long-distance nature of the collaboration, or maybe simply giving the disturbing and cool effect of the band performing on the wing of a plane), Jelinek fired up his laptop and brought the hitherto-dead room to life with atmospheric ambience. Triosk’s drummer began rattling out a building loop in doubletime on the hats, their double bass player brought in a lazy, repetetive bassline while the keyboard player augmented the soundscape with dreamy rolls of lushness. The audience held its breath as slowly the quartet built upon what they’d started, rising and rising and rising until everything broke away and all that was left was Jelinek’s scratchy sounds once more, which died away. A brief pause and then applause. Screams, even. Triosk meets Jan Jelinek rescued the evening, with lush, textured soundscapes that transported you to other places.


I think the main problem with the night was the context in which it was presented. This abstract form of music doesn’t necessarily lend itself well to the gig paradigm – all the bands were up on stage and all the audience’s focus was on them when perhaps it might have been better to divert attention away from the musicians, and perhaps to a certain extent the music. Abstract music like this needs to be presented well or else it just comes off as boring, which sadly was the case for much of this night.
