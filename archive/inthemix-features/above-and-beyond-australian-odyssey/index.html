Above & Beyond: Australian odyssey

Angus Paterson · 15 September 2011 · inthemix

Above & Beyond’s first headline Australian tour comes at an important time for the trio; their second artist album Group Therapy dropped in June, and they’ve been hosting stages all over Europe during their summer festival season. Since launching their Anjunabeats label a decade ago, they’ve grown into one of the world’s most successful DJ acts, reflected in the size of their Australian tour.

Touching down for the first show at Metro City in Perth last Friday and then taking the long haul across the country to the Hordern Pavilion in Sydney, they’ve got huge shows to come this weekend in Adelaide, Melbourne and Brisbane, playing two-and-a-half hour sets at each stop.

ITM sat down with Tony McGuinness and Jono Grant on Saturday night, the two members of the trio here for the tour, shortly before they hit the stage at the Hordern Pavilion in Sydney. We talked shop on their Australian odyssey as well as that ‘Trance 2.0’ buzzword they coined earlier this year.

Last year you did have your own stage at Future Music Festival, but this is your own tour. Is that the pinnacle of what you like to do as artists?
Tony: We’ve done a lot of festivals over the years here and obviously played to a lot of people, but really for us the idea is to play the biggest venue in each town and sell it out. And then go to a bigger one, and a bigger one. You know where you are with that. If you’re on a festival bill, you might be playing to a lot of people, but you don’t know how many of them are there to see you. So it’s a lot nicer that people have bought tickets to see you, and we can put on more of a show than you can do at a festival.

Jono: Also you can choose the line-up as well. If you’re playing at a festival, it’s a bit of a willie-waving fest at times. Everyone’s got an hour and a half set. With a show you do yourself, you can sculpt the night the way you want. We brought along two of the best starters from our label and I think that’s important to showcase the new talent. Mat’s not exactly brand new, but he’s young and fresh, and Jaytech’s an amazing DJ as well.

When you get to craft your own night in your own way, does that make a big difference in how much you enjoy it?
Tony: For us, it’s one thing to play for one million people in Brazil. But how many of those people were there to see us and could actually hear it, it’s hard to know. So it’s a nice thing to do, but it’s actually nicer to play places and make it your own night. It’s important to see some progress. And most places we’ve been doing this, America in particular, we’re starting to see the difference. We did two sold-out nights at the Hollywood Palladium; the first one sold out faster than anybody in history.

That’s nice to show you how you’re doing, ‘cause these days you can’t really use record sales as a measure of that. There are people listening but they’ve stolen it from somewhere. Ticket sales are some kind of measure of progress. Plus, as Jono was saying, you can sculpt the night with the other acts that are playing, so you’re not coming on after someone who’s playing at 142-BPM like we did in Romania a couple of weeks ago. That makes it difficult to do the kind of journey we want to.

Both of your labels seem to be in a sweet spot in terms of the creative output, while the rest of the trance scene might be not quite as consistent as a couple of years ago. What’s the secret for you guys as far as that’s concerned?
Jono: I think it’s quality control. That’s the key thing. If you let the standards slip and sign anything that you think will sell, or you think the audience will want, then there’s a problem. You’ve got to sign stuff that you really like and would play in your set. That sound has changed over the years, and that’s why the sound the sound of the label isn’t the same as it was. It will continually evolve. It won’t be the same next year.

You’re happy to keep it changing?
Tony: It’s interesting. It’s not like we’re consciously changing it. The producers make the records that we sign. But there has been a huge change in what people think of as trance music, which I think is great. I rather foolishly in an interview a few months ago I said, “Trance 2.0”. Then I thought, why did I say that? If you think about it, it started in maybe ’92 with the old Esperanza sound and it’s been through so many incarnations, if we’re on anything in that limited number we’re at least 9.0, if not trance 2 million, 700 thousand and 56.

Because really it evolves a record at a time. It’s not like there’s a memo that goes out that says, “Okay this week’s tempo is 134, and a little bit bassline”. It just happens naturally. Over the last three years, basslines have started to get a little more adventurous and more a part of the track than they were ten years ago. That’s continued, tempos have come down and there’s more elements from other music – particularly house. House I’d say at the moment is probably ‘Trance 8.0’ - the one that the Swedes are playing now.

We were so scared of riffs a few years ago that we kind of left them in a cupboard and the house guys discovered them and were like, “Oh my god, all this stuff we can make great records out of!” I think that was a wake-up call. It’s a huge mixing pot, like it was at the beginning. There weren’t the tight genres that people impose on the scene now. I mean, one of the first questions you ask when you hear a new record is pathetic really: “What is this? What kind of music is this?” We all naturally want to do that, but people don’t do that in rock music – like, “Oh no, the new U2 single is a ballad”. It’s just a U2 single.

It should be a lot more free I think. It’s tougher to get stuff that works together because everyone’s working off their own hymn sheet now. It’s not as formulaic as it was five years ago. But I think that’s a good thing. I think the basslines and slower tempo make it easier for other people to get into, and easier to dance to.

Jono: I think genres are just a smoke-screen personally. People should listen to music and evaluate on what they’re hearing. I like some house records, I like some classical music; it seems to me that there’s a particular thing with trance music, because it’s considered a dirty word by some parts of the music industry it has a real problem with identity. No one complains when house music changes, but with trance people hang onto 1998 too much or something.

Tony: There are so many subtle changes record to record, it seems odd to me to be worrying about what it used to be or what it is now.

Jono: That’s an interesting thing about dance music. Dance music is a lot about copying and taking ideas from other people. That sort of thing is very incestuous compared to other styles. Obviously in pop music, they borrow a lot of ideas, but in dance music it’s also about making a record that’ll fit in with other records. It’s also about hopefully making a statement and pushing things forward. Then occasionally you’ll get a record that kicks the ball a lot further down the field, if you like.

Tony: It may be a little bit less true than it used to be, but dance music is the one kind of music where records are designed to be played two at a time. So you can’t completely change the rules. You have to have something that’s going to fit into a DJ set somewhere; it’s got to have a groove and a bassline and a sonic range that’s going to sound okay played on top of another one. It’s like a baton race. It happens in incremental steps. What’s happened in our scene at least is that there have been enough of those to get us to a much broader area. But for us the most important thing is can we still do what we do within that, which is convey emotion, songs, lyrics, and try to maintain the same emotional delivery.

You’re a marquee act now at the top of bill. Is there a tension between trying to push the sound forward and pleasing tens of thousands of people?
Tony: There definitely is. It becomes an issue. There’s a well-known cliché in the music business of the difficult second album. The first incarnation of Above & Beyond was done with a blue sky and a green field. There was no expectation. Now there is expectation, so there’s disappointment when you do something different. But as an artist you’ve got to change and evolve. If Madonna was still doing Holiday 30 years later, everyone would think she was a dodo. It is in the nature of artists to want to develop and change.

Jono: I feel that way about some artists I grew up with, like the Pet Shop Boys. I liked their ‘80s stuff but to be honest I could take or leave what they did from the ‘90s on. But I still respect them. There’s a very interactive audience with our music, and they almost want you to make music to order. You can’t contrive these things. You’ve got to do what you want to do and hopefully then someone will like it – in that order. You don’t write music for your fans unfortunately, as arrogant and nasty as that sounds. People write music to express themselves and that’s the way it is.

So what are you trying to create tonight?
Tony: Well, it’s our first time in Australia for a year or so. So we’re trying to be quite inclusive in what we play. Rather than just play this week’s set, you have to acknowledge that there are a lot of records that have come out in the last 12 months from us and others that people haven’t heard us play. The nice thing about it is that it’s not a festival set, we’ve been doing a lot of those in summer. You get an hour to play all the big hits. I understand why it’s like that, but it does get a little bit tedious as a DJ. So we’ve got two and a half hours with quite a lot of new stuff we’ve put in just this week. New tracks from Nitrous Oxide and Sunny Lax; they’re not quite finished yet, but enough to play out. We’re sort of A&R-ing them on the road. With festival sets, everyone’s just playing the hits.

Jono: There’s less room for experimentation. If I really wanted to go see an artist, I’d personally rather see them on their own night. If you want to go to a buffet and have lots of things, a festival is great for that. But I’m more into the idea of going somewhere for one reason.

Tony: I actually went to Global Gathering this year as a punter, ‘cause Jono and Paavo were doing the gig and I had the day off. So I went with my girlfriend and we tried to see all the people we wanted to see, so the most time I spent in one tent was 15 minutes. ‘Cause we wanted to see Dubfire, then Underworld over there, then Joris Voorn, then obviously a bit of Above & Beyond and Richie Hawtin. The amount of time we spent walking around between things, you don’t see that much music actually. Skream and Benga were amazing actually, saw about 15 minutes of that.
