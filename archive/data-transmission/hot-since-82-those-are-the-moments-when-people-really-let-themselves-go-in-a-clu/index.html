Hot Since 82: “Those are the moments when people really let themselves go in a club”

Angus Paterson · 13 December 2013 · Data Transmission

Hot Since 82: “Those are the moments when people really let themselves go in a club”

Hot Since 82 has been the single hottest act to rise through the ranks in house music this year; interestingly though, he’s been around for a fair while longer than people might realise. Leeds-based producer Daley Padley has a history in DJing and production that goes back nearly 15 years, with early successes taking him as far afield as residencies in Ibiza.

The classic house sound that defined his early career ultimately led to him disconnecting, as his passion for the music waned. However, the creative fire was reignited several years ago when he was lured back by the charms of underground house and techno. Hot Since 82 was born.

It’s not hard to understand why he’s stood out so much in 2013. Padley’s sound is an extremely polished variant on modern tech house, though the appeal comes though from how direct, punchy and laser-focused his grooves are; it’s underground house engineered for the big rooms. And it was showcased to full effect on his Little Black Book release; an album/compilation fusion that came packing with a fresh stash of Hot Since 82 peaktime destroyers that have crawled their way into the record boxes of a vast number of DJs.

As Padley himself puts it (though without even a hint of cocky self-assuredness): “Guy Gerber plays them, Sasha plays them, Steve Lawler has played them. Pete Tong plays it. I can’t think of any DJ that hasn’t been playing at least one of the records”.

When Data Transmission speaks to Padley, he conveys an air of relaxed contentment, as an artist who’s achieved what he has just by doing what comes naturally; rather than via any kind of calculated path to fame. “All I can say is that I’m in a very, very good place. In my career, financially and personally as well. That’s something that money can’t buy,” he said.

Data Transmission finds out more about the massive year that’s been had by Hot Since 82, and what he’s got coming up in the next 12 months including his hotly anticipated performance alongside Sasha and Ellen Allien for Last Night on Earth at London’s Brixton Academy this New Years Eve.

What’s your schedule like at the moment?

Thursday I was in Madrid, Friday in Edinburgh, Saturday I was at the Warehouse Project, and Sunday I was in Dublin. I got home last night and I’m pretty exhausted. It’s been like this for a good year now. The problem is this weekend I partied a little bit, so it took it out of me [laughs].

It’s all happened really quickly, but at the same time I’ve been doing this Hot Since 82 thing for nearly three years now. It started off quite calm and gradual, but the past year has been pretty mental. I think the key is to keep plugging away, and just to keep making good music. I’ve never had a secret formula for taking over the world or anything like that. I’ve just been looking to keep the momentum up with putting good music out there.

Your history as a DJ/producer goes a bit further back, though the adoption of the Hot Since 82 alias marked somewhat of a transition for you?

I’ve been playing music for 14 years; I’m 31 at the minute. And I got into playing house music, the classic sound of Kerri Chandler or Roger Sanchez back in the day. It was only around 2004 when I first went to Circo Loco in Ibiza that I started getting into more underground sounds. Deep house, tech house, techno especially. So I had another couple of years playing my classic sound, but I was listening personally to techno and going to techno clubs, while still making more of a classic sound in my productions. And it just got too much at the end of the day. As you get older as well, your sound does mature, and I’m a big music guy anyway, I’m just a music head in general. And I just wasn’t enjoying the music I was making or DJing anymore, and I’m the kind of person where if 100 percent love is not involved, then there is no point. So I took a couple of years out, found a new sound and then here we are now.

At what point did you decide you wanted to step back into things and start making a contribution again?

I didn’t have a plan. I wasn’t prepared to start DJing, or officially making music and sending it to record labels again. I moved into the city centre of Leeds, I got a new apartment and bought some new studio gear. And basically, I just stated going raving with my friends again, going out Friday and Saturday and coming in on a Monday or a Tuesday [laughs]. I kind of started reliving my youth again in a really childish way. I was just going out and getting wasted again, going to techno clubs and getting soaked into the new sound that was coming around, this deep house kind of stuff. I produced this track called Let It Ride, and I just had it sitting around on my hard drive for a few months. I played it to my mates, and they were like, ‘this is amazing, you should sign it’, and I was like, ‘naaaah’. And they were like, ‘seriously, you should sign this record.’ I already had a relationship with the Noir Music label, we had been friends since back in the day so it seemed like an obvious choice to give it to them, because I knew their label was doing well, and we were friends. They said this is great, and then before I know it it’s #3 on Beatport, and people are wanting to book me, and people are wanting remixes. And here we are now.

Was it a fairly quick trajectory after that?

It was just like ‘boom’ after Let It Ride. It got to #3, so I thought oh, maybe I should set a SoundCloud up, start putting some mixes on it. The mixes starting getting some really mad love. Then the remixes started coming in… it’s just been crazy. This year though has been kind of the ‘rise to the top’, so to speak.

It must satisfying that this ‘rise to the top’ was never really planned.

Exactly, and I think that’s why it’s been so successful. You could ask my friends, my manager or any of the record labels I’ve worked with, I’m a very fluid, organic guy. I do talk a lot, but I’m not short of anything. I was quite happy and content with my life as it was already, I had lots of good friends and family around me, so I wasn’t soul searching. And this is why I’m so proud that it’s done in this direction, because it’s all been so organic and natural. That shows in the music as well; nothing is forced, and nothing is rushed. It is what it is, and people have really, really gotten into it. So I’m happy that I haven’t kissed anybody’s ass, I haven’t rubbed anybody up the wrong way. I’m just here, I’m just doing my thing and I’m happy.

When I do arrive back home in the UK, I’m not the kind that’s sitting on Skype talking to other DJs or connecting. Because I know a lot of guys like to do that, to ‘network’ so to speak, and I don’t really do that, because I like the time that I do have, I like spending it with my friends and my family. I like a bit of cut off. Obviously being in the studio is another thing, but ultimately when I’m at home, I wanna lay on the sofa and watch TV, I wanna do normal things.

There’s something quite unique about your sound, which I think is why people have picked up on it so much. What do you see the appeal as yourself?

The main thing I think is it has all the amazing, wicked elements of house music, all in the one seven-minute record. There’s progressive elements, it’s very melodic. It has a big bassline, the kinda deep house groove with a bit more grit. It has the rolling percussion, it has some old school 808 and 909 hats. And it has breakdowns. I know that if I’m on the dancefloor, I want it to build and I want it to take you somewhere. Because that’s the power of house music, it has the ability to do nothing but take you everywhere. So I try to implement that kind of factor in each kind of record. It really sits on the fence between being underground and cool, and not mainroom as such, but that little bit more danceable I guess.

The thing that stands out for me is it all has a very driving, pumping kind of energy.

That’s ultimately when people let themselves go in a club, when there’s that kind of reach-for-the-lasers, fist-pumping element to it. I try to implement that, but not take it too high.

What sort of direction do you see your sound developing in?

Ultimately I want to work with a lot more vocalists and songwriters, I want to put a bit more time into having songs. I don’t mean in a commercial sense, but most of my songs are instrumentals unless they’re remixes. It’s extremely hard to find a good session singer, and a good top writer too, in order to get a good jam finished. So I just want to work with some key people that I’m already in touch with and networking with, and continue to do big remixes; and I never want to turn my back on those who have already supported me. Because I see this a lot, people blow up and then before you know it, their sound has changed quite rapidly. And I don’t want to do that, I just want to keep it there, but also evolving.

Your Little Black Book compilation would have been one of the big projects for you at the end of this year. How long were you working on this one for?

That was a funny one, because the excessive touring can make things quite difficult when you want to get in the studio. Ultimately about seven months. I would have liked to have spent a bit more time doing a bit more original stuff, but crammed in that is four or five shows a week, the travelling, the remixes that weren’t part of the Little Black Book project. And I’ve got a private life as well. So it’s been a massive job getting it all finished, but ultimately it is finished, it is done and it’s out there, and it’s selling really well. There’s some key moments on there, The End, Insane I Know and Shadows, there’s some good stuff on there that I’m really proud of.

Nearly every single track on there is a belter of an anthem, of some description.

That’s what we want to hear [laughs].

Were you attracted to the compilation/album format, rather than going straight down the artist album route, and delivering something a lot more focussed in the clubs?

Yeah. It’s a mix compilation, but the guys at the Moda Black label wanted to do something specifically with an artist where they could showcase some new releases, some collaborations etcetera. So it’s not my debut album, I kind of have that in my head already and we’ll probably see it in the next year or so. But I think it’s a wicked format, and it’s nothing too serious either. And the Moda Black guys are personal friends of mine as well, and we’re all looked after by the same management, so it’s been a nice fluid thing to work with these guys, they all work extremely hard at the label. And I think it’s just a good concept; you can purchase all the individual tracks as well, you can buy the comp as a physical CD. We’ve got all the grounds covered there. And going on the response, I think people are really feeling it.

Something the Moda Black guys commented on when it came out is how the complexity of your arrangements stands out. The appeal of the tunes might be direct and upfront, but there’s detail in there that reveals itself on subsequent listens.

If you listen the Green Velvet Bigger Than Prince remix, you think ‘oh right this is simple’. A weird synth, some big bass, drums and it’s done. But if you listen to it on some decent headphones, you can hear all the atmospheric things that are pinned underneath, the little stabs that you might not necessarily pick up on in the clubs. But that’s also the Hot Since 82 sound I guess; I don’t know how to explain it, it is what it is.

Tell us about the album that we’ll hopefully see over the next year.

There are specific artists that I’m going to work with, and I’m always writing titles down for tracks, as well as around a thousand little ideas jotted down during all the travelling that I do. So it’s just a matter of putting all of those into motion. I’m not in a rush to get it all done, I’m going to launch the record label in January or February so I want to prolong the album and spend as much time on it as possible. I’m just about all nailed up to the summertime with shows, and all of next year is pretty much all planned out, so I might not have time to start on the album until later in the year anyway. I’m thinking of going over to the States and spending a bit of time in New York getting it done there, to lock myself away in a different city, because when I’m at home there’s a lot of distractions. So the album might come out in around 2015, maybe around March time.

So what’s the plans for the record label next year?

I’m working on the first original track now, and we’ve got a few cool remixes on board. I’ve got a few tracks that I’ve not signed yet, but hopefully I’m gonna. So it’s just gonna be another outlet for me to put some more of my stuff out, and hopefully bring some new boys up as well. I’m probably going to put around five releases out in the first year and then see how we go, but it’s something that I’ve always wanted to do, and I think I’m in a good place now. I’ve got some good guys onboard that are going to help really well with the label. I can’t tell you too much right now, so you’re just going to have to keep your eyes peeled and look out for it.
