Joss Whedon’s Firefly [Complete Series]

Angus Paterson · 16 August 2004 · inthemix

Director: Joss Whedon.
Cast: Nathan Fillion, Gina Torres, Alan Tudyk.
Distributor: 20th Century Fox.
Classification: M15+
Running Time: 14 episodes.

The world of television needs more great minds like Joss Whedon. Responsible for some of the best TV of the last decade with Buffy the Vampire Slayer and Angel, writer/director/producer Whedon has consistently delivered action-packed and accessible television, high in intelligence and served with healthy lashings of post-modern wit. Firefly was another of Whedon's forays into TV land, and was a sci-fi series that centred on the intergalactic exploits of the crew of the spaceship Serenity. Unfortunately, Firefly bombed on landing and was quickly removed from the network's schedule, but the show gained enough of a cult following for Whedon to be able to get a full-length feature film into production. Not surprisingly, none of the local television networks possessed the smarts to pick it up the show and put it on our screens - but Australian Joss Whedon fans can rejoice, because they can now purchase the entire season of Firefly on DVD prior to the film being released over the Christmas period.

While Buffy and Angel consisted of an attractive collection of vampires, demons and horror, Firefly is something altogether different: set 500 years in the future where mankind is gradually pushing out into the outer reaches of space to colonise the galaxies, a new frontier is developing. The crew of the ship Serenity are a bunch of petty criminals who move around from planet to planet, picking up contracts whenever they can and pulling jobs of a distinctly questionable nature. The ship's captain Mal and the rest of the crew-members were veteran soldiers who fought in the failed war of independence, and passively they resist the totalitarian Alliance regime that is gradually setting itself up across the galaxy. But when they take onboard renegade medical student Simon and his traumatised sister River who is of great value to the Alliance, things become a tad more complicated. The outlaw crew of Serenity find themselves on the run, and heading into all types of complicated situations.

In a similar fashion to how Buffy the Vampire Slayer skilfully blended horror with more traditional high-school drama, Whedon takes a similar approach with Firefly. Though it's a sci-fi show, Firefly eschews the geeky sci-fi babble of shows like Star Trek and Babylon 5, and infuses it instead with a definite Western sensibility. It's classic post-modern stuff: the method of taking one established set of conventions and merging them with another, drawing the audience's attention to the process at all times. The visual environment established in Firefly is of a frontier land in space, and Whedon brilliantly contrasts the sci-fi imagery with wild-wild-west iconography. It's a future where humans are spreading out to colonise the surrounding galaxies, and this sets it up immediately for a parallel with the Wild West of America's past. Whedon is forever clashing the two thematic styles up against each other; in doing so he skilfully evokes the clichés, plays around with them, before again turning them on their head and subverting the audience's expectations.

All of the familiar elements from the classic westerns you watched as a child are present in Firefly: cowboys, rogues, gunslingers and... line dancing. The wise old preacher is among the crew of the Serenity, as is the town prostitute. But in Whedon's own unique take on the genre, the preacher is someone who offers wisdom to the bandits, while the prostitute is someone who actually demands a high degree of respect. And the respective imagery of the two genres are contrasted endlessly - the universe of Firefly is one where spaceships are shown alongside gunslingers, in a combination that is nothing if not unique. The spaceship shown sweeping over a cattle run during the opening credits says it all, and the great trains of the Wild West are still there to be held to ransom by the bandits - instead now they hover just like Michael J. Fox's skateboard in Back to the Future II rather than running on train tracks. While Firefly is markedly different from anything Whedon has ever done before, his influence can still be seen to pervade every aspect of the show. The same razor-sharp wit and subtle character development of his other shows is again present here, and time is taken to develop the intricate plotlines over successive episodes. It's just a shame that he was only given 14 episodes in which to express his vision.

All this is brought together with some pretty impressive visual effects that bring together the scope of the Firefly universe. The budget is limited for something set on such a large scale, and the show does occasionally suffer from it; but those interested in great writing, an original setting and just damn good television will easily be able to overlook this. Sometimes the Wild West theme may make you cringe a little - line dancing in particular is never good, in any form or any context. Whatsoever. But these occasional transgressions don't change the fact that Firefly is straight up excellent TV, well above the standard set by generic legal dramas like Law and Order or forensic investigation pap like CSI. So upon their guaranteed purchase of the entire season of Firefly, all that is left for Australian Joss Whedon fans to do is to sit back and wait for the release of the feature-film at the end of the year, content in the knowledge that their hero will no doubt deliver the goods.

Special Features: Commentaries, deleted scenes, featurettes, auditions, gag reels, Firefly theme

4 out of 5
