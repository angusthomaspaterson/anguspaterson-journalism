Study puts drink spiking myths to bed

Angus Paterson · 16 June 2009 · inthemix

Everyone who is part of the dance community has heard stories about drinks being spiked at nightclubs and parties, but if the results of a new West Australian study are to be believed, the suspected victims of are more likely to be suffering from drugs and alcohol they have willingly consumed themselves. The study published in the Emergency Medicine Australasia journal reviewed 101 suspected cases of drink spiking – and found that not a single case involved the subject being slipped any kind of sedative or illicit drug.

Dr Mark Little, a clinical toxicologist at the Royal Perth Hospital, told News Limited the study points to excessive alcohol and illegal drug use as being at the centre of most drink-spiking claims. “The public’s perception that it’s a guy putting a sedative drug into a woman’s drink, at a pub or a club, we just didn’t find that at all,” he said. “As a community, we have a bigger problem with illicit drug use and alcohol binge drinking than we do with drink spiking.”

Dr Little claims the study did not identify a single case where a sedative drug was likely to have been illegally placed in a drink. “It’s more the case of the girl who likes vodka and orange, let’s give her a double, a triple… Nearly all people were unconcerned with the amount of alcohol they had drank. When we said the average dose was about eight standard drinks … they weren’t concerned about that at all.”

The study looked at 101 suspected victims of drink spiking who were taken to two different Perth hospitals over a period of 19 months. Close to 90 per-cent were female, with a majority aged under 25 years. Around 70 per cent the alleged drink spiking took place at a nightclub or pub, usually within two hours of midnight and on a weekend. The average blood alcohol level was .096 on arrival at hospital, nearly double the legal limit for driving, with more than a quarter having illicit drugs (mainly amphetamines and cannabis), in their system they admitted to taking themselves.

The results reflect the findings of a similar study conducted in inner-city London for the Postgraduate Medical Journal in 2007, which concluded that the use of sedative drugs to spike drinks are not as common as reported in the media.
